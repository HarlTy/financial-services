# Washington State Module

Last verified: 2026-07-28. Sources: WA Dept. of Revenue; RCW 82.87 (capital gains excise); RCW 83.100 (estate tax); ESSB 5813 (2025); ESB 6347 (2026); SB 5291 (2025); ESSB 6346 (2026).

Washington has **no state income tax on wages** — but four WA-specific regimes
routinely change the answer for a WA household, and two of them were amended
within the last 13 months. Never give an estate, capital-gains, or LTC answer to
a WA resident without this file.

Section order follows `_template.md`. Where a section is `VERIFY`, the topic has
not been researched against a primary source — that is not a finding of "none."

## 1. Income tax

**Wages: none.** Washington imposes no income tax on wage income.

**Retirement income: VERIFY.** Not researched. The absence of a wage income tax
does not by itself establish the treatment of pension, IRA, or annuity income,
and this module has not confirmed it.

**Capital gains: a separate excise tax, not an income tax.** RCW 82.87; ESSB 5813
(2025), retroactive to 1/1/2025. *Quinn v. State* (Wash. 2023) upheld it as an
excise rather than an income tax, which is why it survives the state
constitution's uniformity constraints.

- **Rate mechanics** (RCW 82.87.040 as amended by ESSB 5813 sec. 101). Two
  charges apply to the same defined base:
  - **(1)(a) — 7%** of an individual's **"Washington capital gains."**
  - **(1)(b) — an additional 2.90%** of the portion of **Washington capital
    gains** exceeding **$1,000,000**.

  "Washington capital gains" is a **defined term** (RCW 82.87.020, with
  allocation under RCW 82.87.060): long-term gain allocated to Washington, **net
  of the standard deduction**. Both charges operate on that same post-deduction
  figure, so **the $1,000,000 surcharge threshold is measured AFTER the standard
  deduction**, not on gross gain.

  The commonly seen bracket phrasing — 7% up to $1M of post-deduction Washington
  capital gains, 9.9% above it — is **arithmetically identical**, because
  7% + 2.90% = 9.9% on the excess. Use whichever is clearer, but state the base:
  the ambiguity that matters is not the rate, it is what the $1M is measured on.
  On a $278,000 standard deduction the two readings of the threshold differ by
  2.9% × $278,000 = $8,062 — the post-deduction reading above is the correct one.

  The $1M surcharge threshold is **NOT** indexed.
- **Standard deduction:** $278,000 for TY2025, CPI-indexed — VERIFY the TY2026
  amount at DOR before computing.
- **Exempt:** real estate; assets held in retirement accounts;
  livestock/agricultural assets; timber; commercial fishing privileges; plus
  deduction treatment tied to qualified family-owned small business sales —
  VERIFY the exact category fit against RCW 82.87 before relying on an exemption.
- **Who pays:** individuals only, on long-term gains. A single-member LLC is
  disregarded — its gains land on the owner's return.
- **Filing:** returns due April 15 following the tax year (first filings were
  April 15, 2026 for TY2025).
- **Planning notes:** timing gains across years manages both the annual standard
  deduction and the $1M surcharge tier; the retirement-account and real-estate
  exemptions make asset-location choices materially more valuable in WA than in
  most states.

## 2. Estate & inheritance

**Estate tax: yes** (RCW 83.100) — split-year 2026.

- **Deaths 7/1/2025 – 6/30/2026:** exclusion $3,000,000, CPI-indexed
  (≈$3,076,000 for early-2026 deaths — VERIFY the DOR table); top rate **35%** on
  the portion above $9M (ESSB 5813 — briefly the highest state rate in the
  nation).
- **Deaths on/after 7/1/2026:** exclusion $3,000,000 (Seattle-area CPI indexed);
  graduated rates **10%–20%**, top rate 20% (ESB 6347 rollback, signed
  3/24/2026).
- **NO portability between spouses.** An exemption unused at the first death is
  lost. Standard responses: credit-shelter/bypass trust, disclaimer trust, and
  QTIP election planning to preserve both spouses' exclusions — drafting is a hard
  route to the estate attorney.
- **The federal/WA gap:** federal exemption $15M vs WA ~$3M means the WA tax is
  the binding constraint for most affected households; a plan built only around
  the federal number misses the state exposure entirely. A closely held business
  interest can push an estate over $3M quickly.
- **Scale check:** on a $10M estate, a death after 7/1/2026 vs just before saves
  roughly $200,000 from the rate rollback — illustrative; compute against the
  current DOR rate table.

**Inheritance tax: VERIFY.** Not researched. Washington's estate tax is levied on
the estate; whether any separate inheritance tax on beneficiaries exists has not
been confirmed against a primary source in this module. Do not report "none."

## 3. Marital property

- Washington is a community property state. Both halves of community property
  receive a **full basis step-up at the first spouse's death** (IRC §1014(b)(6))
  — the "double step-up," a major income-tax advantage over common-law states and
  a reason to be careful before retitling appreciated community assets as
  separate property.
- Community property agreements (CPAs) can confirm character and secure step-up
  treatment, but they interact with trust-based estate plans — coordinate the CPA
  and any credit-shelter design with the estate attorney rather than treating them
  independently.
- Characterization matters for the business: an LLC interest acquired during
  marriage is presumptively community property — relevant to estate math and to
  any future sale.

## 4. State programs

**WA Cares Fund (LTSS Trust): yes.**

- **Tax:** 0.58% of gross W-2 wages, employee-paid, no wage cap.
  **Self-employment income is NOT subject** unless the person opts in.
- **Benefit:** up to $36,500 lifetime (inflation-adjusted), claimable from
  July 1, 2026.
- **SB 5291 (2025; most provisions 1/1/2026):** out-of-state portability for
  vested contributors and expanded vesting pathways (10-year, or
  3-of-last-6-years).
- The original private-LTC-insurance opt-out window is closed (it required
  coverage in place before 11/1/2021).
- **Planning note:** $36,500 is a partial offset, not long-term-care coverage.
  Real LTC exposure still warrants the traditional-vs-hybrid analysis in
  `../frameworks.md`; product purchase remains a hard route.

**Paid family & medical leave: VERIFY.** Not researched in this module.

**Auto-IRA / state-facilitated retirement savings: VERIFY.** Not researched. §4
covers the LTC program only; nothing here establishes whether a state-facilitated
retirement savings program exists.

## 6. Cross-state interactions

Bilateral content lives in each state's own module (D1). Both items below are
**stubs flagged VERIFY pending the Oregon build**; the WA-side facts are stated,
the OR-side mechanics are not yet authored.

**WA resident with Portland-metro wages.**
mirror: states/oregon.md (Phase 1)
- Oregon taxes a nonresident on wages sourced to Oregon. A WA resident who
  performs work in Oregon has OR-source income regardless of where the household
  lives.
- The Metro Supportive Housing Services (SHS) tax turns on **physical presence
  within the Metro boundary**, not on employer location. Where the employer is
  headquartered does not decide it; where the person actually works does.
- **WA has no income tax, so no credit mechanism exists on the WA side.** This is
  the asymmetry that surprises households: the usual "credit for taxes paid to
  another state" relief has nothing to attach to, because WA levies no comparable
  tax to credit against. The OR liability is not offset — it is simply owed.
- VERIFY before relying on any of it: OR rate and bracket detail, Metro SHS and
  Multnomah PFA thresholds and filing mechanics, and withholding obligations.

**WA ↔ OR relocation year.**
mirror: states/oregon.md (Phase 1)
- A relocation year splits residency, so income must be sourced to the period and
  the state — a single annual figure will not answer it.
- Direction matters asymmetrically. Moving WA → OR brings income into an
  income-taxing state; moving OR → WA leaves one. Gain-realization timing
  interacts with both the WA capital-gains excise (§1) and OR treatment, and the
  favorable year is not the same in both directions.
- VERIFY before relying on any of it: OR part-year residency rules and forms,
  sourcing rules for gains realized near the move date, and whether a gain
  recognized while a WA resident can be reached by OR.

## 8. Advisor questions

Built per `../advisor-meetings.md`; these feed the "Authorities in play" section
of a prep packet.

1. **Estate, for the attorney.** Washington allows no portability, so an unused
   exclusion at the first death is gone. Does our current plan actually preserve
   both exclusions — credit-shelter, disclaimer, or QTIP — and does the answer
   change depending on whether the first death falls before or after 7/1/2026?
2. **Marital property, for the attorney.** Have we confirmed the character of
   assets acquired during the marriage, including the LLC interest, and would a
   community property agreement strengthen or conflict with the trust design we
   have?
3. **Capital gains, for the CPA.** For a planned sale, what does the WA excise
   cost realized in one tax year versus split across two — accounting for both
   the annual standard deduction and the $1M surcharge tier measured
   post-deduction?
4. **Asset location, for the advisor.** Retirement accounts and real estate are
   outside the WA excise. Does that change where we should hold appreciating
   assets, relative to the generic federal answer?
5. **Long-term care, for the advisor.** Given WA Cares pays up to $36,500
   lifetime against a real exposure many multiples of that, what does the
   remaining gap look like, and does self-employment opt-in change it?

## 9. Legislative watch

Mirror in `../guardrails.md`.

- **ESSB 6346:** 9.9% WA tax on income over $1M, effective 2028 — a certified
  repeal initiative (I-645) is on the **November 3, 2026 ballot** and a
  constitutional challenge is pending. Treat as NOT settled; do not build plans
  that assume it either survives or dies. Note this is a proposed **income** tax
  and a distinct instrument from the capital-gains excise surcharge in §1, which
  happens to share the 9.9% figure — do not conflate them.
- WA capital-gains and estate parameters changed twice in 13 months. Re-verify
  statute text and DOR tables in every session where they drive a conclusion.
