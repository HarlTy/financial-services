## 1. The question underneath the question

"Should I buy an indexed annuity" is a product question. The planning question is: **how much of your essential spending needs to be covered by income that doesn't depend on markets, and what's the cheapest way to cover it?** Once that number exists, the product either fills a real gap or it doesn't. Answering it in the other order is how people end up owning a solution to a problem they never sized.

So the sequence is: essential (non-discretionary) annual spending → guaranteed income you already have or can get → the gap → and only then, what fills the gap.

## 2. Check the annuity you already own first

Delaying Social Security is, functionally, buying an inflation-adjusted lifetime annuity from the cheapest issuer available to you — and my frameworks reference describes it in exactly those terms as a sequence-risk mitigation.

- Delayed credits accrue at roughly **8%/year from full retirement age to 70**, landing **24–32% above your primary insurance amount** depending on cohort. FRA is **67 for anyone born 1960 or later**; earliest claim 62. (2026 SSA figures, FINAL.)
- The 2026 COLA is **2.8%** (FINAL) — the point being that the increase applies to the whole benefit, indefinitely, which is the feature commercial products charge the most to approximate.
- **Survivor optimization:** the higher earner's record becomes the survivor benefit. For an MFJ household, the higher earner delaying is longevity insurance for *both* of you — and the widow's-penalty exposure (survivor files single at compressed brackets) is a real planning item, not a footnote.
- Breakeven for delaying typically lands in the low-to-mid 80s, but longevity expectations and survivor needs dominate that decision, not breakeven arithmetic alone.

If there is unclaimed delay available in your household, that capacity is generally the first floor-building dollar to spend, and it's spent by *not* claiming rather than by writing a check.

## 3. Three ways to buy an income floor

Presented as alternatives with tradeoffs, not a ranking, because the right mix depends on your gap size and your tolerance for variable spending.

**(a) Delay Social Security; fund the bridge years from the portfolio.**
Cheapest inflation-linked floor, no counterparty risk beyond the federal program, fully liquid until spent. Costs you portfolio dollars in the bridge window and requires that the portfolio can carry that draw.
*Assumption: sufficient portfolio balance to fund spending from claim-delay start to age 70 without forcing sales in a down market.*

**(b) Build the floor from your own bonds: 1–3 years' spending in cash/short duration plus a bond or TIPS ladder covering the early retirement window.**
This is the sequence-of-returns mitigation my frameworks reference lists first, and it's the direct structural competitor to an insured product. Transparent pricing, retains liquidity and legacy value, no surrender period. It does *not* insure longevity — a ladder ends, a lifetime contract doesn't.
*Assumption: you accept longevity risk on the far end, or plan to revisit an insured solution later at older-age pricing.*

**(c) Insure the residual gap with a contractual lifetime income product** (indexed being one design among several).
Genuinely solves what (a) and (b) can't: income that cannot be outlived, and a floor that doesn't depend on your own discipline in a bad market. The cost is liquidity, complexity, and pricing you cannot easily verify. My insurance framework's governing principle transfers here: **permanent products for permanent needs only** — the case has to rest on a permanent need (lifetime income you'd otherwise be unable to guarantee), not on a return story.
*Assumption: the gap is real after (a) and (b), and the contract is priced competitively — which is precisely what I cannot verify from my references.*

On the "return story" point, my operating rules forbid me from forecasting, and that cuts specifically against the usual indexed-annuity sales frame: any illustration of index-linked crediting is a projection, not a promise. Treat "market upside with no downside" as a *pricing* claim to be examined, not a feature to be accepted — the downside protection is paid for by the caps, spreads, and participation limits I can't see.

## 4. Sizing benchmark for the gap

To know whether you have a gap at all, you need the portfolio's sustainable draw:

- **Bengen (1994):** 4% initial withdrawal plus inflation adjustments survived every historical 30-year U.S. period; his later work put the historical worst-case maximum nearer 4.7%.
- **Morningstar, *State of Retirement Income*, 2025 edition (Dec 2025):** base-case **starting** safe withdrawal rate of **3.9%** — 30-year horizon, roughly 30–50% equity, 90% success, forward-looking return assumptions.
- Horizon matters: roughly 5% supportable over 20 years, ~7% over 15.

These are planning signals, not guarantees. If the gap between essential spending and (Social Security + any pension) is comfortably inside a 3.9–4% draw, the case for buying insured income is mostly about *behavior and certainty*, not necessity. If the gap exceeds it, that's the strongest legitimate argument for a contractual floor — and also the case where overpaying for it hurts most.

One more calibration: **dynamic withdrawal methods** (Guyton-Klinger guardrails, RMD-style, VPW-type amortization) trade steadier failure risk for variable spending. They are the free alternative to an insured floor when *some* spending flexibility exists. If your essential spending is genuinely rigid, they don't help and the annuity case strengthens.

## 5. Funding interactions — where an otherwise-correct decision goes wrong

The purchase is a taxable event in disguise depending on where the money comes from. All of these are mandatory checks in my operating rules for any income-spike scenario:

- **Funding from a taxable account** means realizing long-term gains. In Washington that hits the **capital-gains excise tax** (RCW 82.87): **7%** of "Washington capital gains," plus an additional **2.90%** on the portion above **$1,000,000** — and critically, **the $1M threshold is measured after the standard deduction**, not on gross gain. Standard deduction was **$278,000 for TY2025**, CPI-indexed; **the TY2026 amount is VERIFY — confirm at WA DOR before computing anything.** A single-member LLC is disregarded, so its gains land on your return.
- **NIIT:** 3.8% on net investment income above MAGI **$200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ** (TY2026, FINAL) — **not indexed**, so it creeps up on households every year.
- **IRMAA, two years forward, if either spouse will be 63+ in the funding year:** 2026 premiums are based on **2024 MAGI** (two-year lookback), assessed **per person**, and each tier is a **cliff** — $1 over triggers the full surcharge. First tier begins at MAGI **$109,000 single / $218,000 MFJ** (FINAL). Part B standard premium is **$202.90/month** in 2026 (FINAL); surcharges add **$81.20–$487.00/month** (VERIFY — secondary source) and Part D adds **$14.50–$91.00/month** (VERIFY).
- **ACA, if anyone is pre-65:** the enhanced premium tax credits **expired 12/31/2025** and subsidy cliffs are back. A one-year income spike to fund a contract can cost real premium dollars.
- **Social Security taxation, if claiming:** combined income = AGI + tax-exempt interest + ½ benefits; MFJ thresholds **$32,000 / $44,000**, **not indexed** (FINAL).
- **Funding from an IRA or 401(k)** avoids the realization event but keeps the money inside the RMD regime — **RMD beginning age 73 (born 1951–1959) or 75 (born 1960+)** (FINAL). How a deferred-income contract inside an IRA interacts with RMD calculation is a real and technical question that my reference set does not cover; it goes to your CPA, not to a sales illustration.
- **Pre-59½ money** carries the early-distribution tax under **IRC §72(t)**.

## 6. Washington-specific overlay

- **No state income tax on wages.**
- **Retirement and annuity income: not researched in my Washington module — flagged VERIFY.** I want to be exact here, because this is the cell most likely to be assumed away: Washington's lack of a *wage* income tax does **not** by itself establish the treatment of pension, IRA, or annuity income, and my module has not confirmed it. Do not let anyone (including me) tell you "annuity income is tax-free in WA" as a selling point without a primary-source check.
- **Assets held in retirement accounts are exempt from the WA capital-gains excise**, while taxable accounts are not — which makes the *location* of the funding dollars worth more in WA than in most states.
- **Legislative watch:** ESSB 6346 (2026) would impose a 9.9% WA tax on income over $1M effective 2028; a repeal initiative (I-645) is on the **November 3, 2026** ballot and a constitutional challenge is pending. **Treat as unsettled** — do not lock a multi-decade income structure to an assumption that it either survives or dies. (Note this is a proposed *income* tax, distinct from the capital-gains excise surcharge that happens to share the 9.9% figure.)

## 7. Who is recommending this to you

This matters more with annuities than with almost anything else, because of how they're distributed.

- **Standard of care:** an RIA owes fiduciary duty at all times; a broker-dealer owes Regulation Best Interest (**17 C.F.R. §240.15l-1**) — stronger than old suitability, weaker than full fiduciary duty; hybrid/dually registered advisors **switch standards by account type**. Ask which standard applies to *this specific recommendation*, in writing.
- **Explicit red flag from my advisor-evaluation reference:** *product pitches (annuities, insurance) before a written plan exists.* Also on that list: vague fee answers, performance promises, and pressure or artificial urgency. If the annuity showed up before a plan document did, that sequence is itself the finding.
- **Verify the person:** Form ADV Part 2 and Form CRS, plus SEC IAPD and FINRA BrokerCheck, before engaging.
- **Cost frame:** median blended AUM fee ≈1% up to $1M, but all-in cost (advisory fee + fund expenses + platform) averages ≈**1.65%** (Kitces/Veres). Convert everything — including the annuity's internal costs and rider charges — to **all-in dollars per year** before comparing anything to anything.

## Take to your advisor

**The decision to be made:** whether any portion of your essential retirement spending needs a contractual lifetime income floor, and if so, whether an indexed design — versus a simpler income annuity, a bond/TIPS ladder, or additional Social Security delay — is the cheapest way to buy that floor.

**Inputs used here:** default household lens (MFJ, WA resident, W-2 + LLC income, minor children); 2026 federal figures and Washington statutes as cited; no ages, balances, spending target, or Social Security estimates supplied. Anything age-dependent above (IRMAA at 63+, ACA pre-65, RMD age, claiming strategy) is unresolved until you supply ages.

**Bring:** your essential-vs-discretionary spending split, Social Security statements from ssa.gov for both spouses, account balances by tax bucket (taxable / tax-deferred / Roth / HSA), and — if a specific contract is on the table — **the full illustration plus the contract disclosure and surrender schedule**, not the brochure.

**Questions that resolve it:**

1. What is my essential annual spending, what guaranteed income do I already have, and what is the gap in dollars — before we discuss any product?
2. Have we exhausted Social Security delay first, including the survivor-benefit effect of the higher earner delaying to 70? What does the household claiming analysis show?
3. Show me the gap covered three ways — SS delay, a bond/TIPS ladder plus cash bucket, and this contract — priced in all-in dollars per year for each. Which is cheapest per dollar of guaranteed income?
4. On this specific contract: what are the cap, participation rate, spread, and rider charges; can the insurer change them after issue; what is the surrender schedule; and what is the total cost in dollars per year?
5. Are you a fiduciary for this recommendation, will you put that in writing, and what compensation do you or your firm receive on this product versus the alternatives in question 3?
6. **For the CPA:** which funding source is cheapest after tax — and if we sell taxable positions, what does the WA capital-gains excise cost under **RCW 82.87**, netting the standard deduction (TY2026 amount to be verified at DOR) and checking the $1M surcharge tier measured post-deduction? Also confirm NIIT position (**IRC §1411**) and, if either of us is 63+, the IRMAA effect two years forward (**42 U.S.C. §1395r(i)** — per person, cliff pricing).
7. **For the CPA:** how is annuity income treated for Washington purposes — confirmed against a primary source, not inferred from the absence of a wage income tax? And if this sits inside an IRA, how does it interact with RMDs under **IRC §401(a)(9)** and the 2024 final regulations?
8. What happens to this contract if I die first, if my spouse dies first, or if we need the principal in year six?
