# Planning Frameworks & Worked Processes

Last verified: 2026-07-28.

## CFP Board 7-step financial planning process
1. Understand the client's personal and financial circumstances → 2. Identify and select goals → 3. Analyze the current course of action and alternatives → 4. Develop recommendations → 5. Present recommendations → 6. Implement → 7. Monitor progress and update. Use this as the spine of any full-plan session. Practitioner firms run the same loop as discovery → plan development → implementation → ongoing review.

## Retirement readiness benchmarks (Fidelity framework)
Save at least 1× income by 30, 3× by 40, 6× by 50, 8× by 60, 10× by 67, assuming ~15% total savings rate including employer match. The framework targets savings replacing ~45% of pre-retirement income with Social Security covering the rest (~80% total replacement). Treat these as calibration points, not verdicts — adjust for pensions, planned retirement age, and actual spending.

## Safe withdrawal rates (SWR)
- Bengen (1994): 4% initial withdrawal + annual inflation adjustments survived every historical 30-year U.S. period; his later work put the historical worst-case maximum nearer 4.7%.
- Trinity study (1998): corroborated for stock-heavy portfolios.
- Morningstar, *State of Retirement Income* 2025 edition (Dec 2025): base-case STARTING SWR of 3.9% (30-year horizon, roughly 30–50% equity, 90% success, forward-looking return assumptions). Check each annual edition at refresh.
- Horizon effect: roughly 5% supportable over 20 years, ~7% over 15 (same historical logic).
- Dynamic methods trade steadier failure-risk for variable spending: Guyton-Klinger guardrails (cut/raise withdrawals at portfolio bands), RMD-style (divide balance by remaining life expectancy), amortization-based (VPW-type). Offer a dynamic option whenever a plan shows either a high failure rate or a large projected surplus — both are signals the static rule is the wrong tool.

## Sequence-of-returns risk
Poor returns in the ~5 years before/after retirement do disproportionate, sometimes permanent damage even when average returns end up fine. Mitigations: 1–3 years of spending in cash/short duration, flexible spending rules, a bond/TIPS ladder covering the early window, part-time income, and delaying Social Security (an inflation-adjusted annuity purchased by waiting).

## Bucket strategy
Bucket 1 cash (1–3 years' spending) → Bucket 2 bonds/income (years ~3–10) → Bucket 3 growth. Refill opportunistically after good years. It is a behavioral tool as much as a math tool — reconcile bucket totals with the overall target allocation so the buckets don't silently change portfolio risk.

## Monte Carlo interpretation
Success rate = share of simulated paths that never exhaust the portfolio. 80–90% is a common comfort band; a 100% result usually signals over-saving or under-spending rather than safety. Results are highly sensitive to return and inflation inputs — always name the assumptions (Operating rule 6) and prefer ranges over a single run.

## Asset location
Tax-deferred: taxable bonds, REITs, high-turnover strategies. Roth: highest-expected-growth assets. Taxable: broad-index equities (qualified dividends, LTCG rates, loss-harvesting capability, basis step-up potential — and in Washington, note that retirement accounts are exempt from the WA capital-gains excise tax while taxable accounts are not, which raises the stakes of location decisions). Municipal bonds carry no state-tax advantage for WA residents (no state income tax); compare after-tax yields on the federal exemption alone.

## Withdrawal sequencing in retirement
Default order: taxable → tax-deferred → Roth. Usually better: bracket management — fill the low brackets each year from tax-deferred money (withdrawals or Roth conversions) while spending from taxable, preserving Roth for late-life, IRMAA-sensitive, and legacy purposes. Model both; the blended approach typically wins for households facing a future RMD bulge.

## Roth conversion playbook (worked process)
1. **Identify the window:** years between retirement (or an income drop) and RMD age (73/75) — and, if flexible, before Social Security starts.
2. **Compute headroom:** target bracket ceiling (12/22/24%) minus projected taxable income = conversion capacity. Cross-foot the resulting tax bill and identify the payment source (paying tax from taxable funds preserves more of the conversion's value).
3. **Interaction checks (mandatory, per SKILL.md rule 3):** IRMAA two years forward if 63+ in the conversion year (per-person cliff); NIIT (conversion income is not NII itself but raises MAGI over the $200k/$250k gates for other investment income); the WA capital-gains standard deduction if gains are also being realized; ACA premiums if pre-65 (enhanced credits expired 12/31/2025 — cliffs are back); taxation of Social Security benefits if claiming.
4. **Motivations that strengthen the case:** a future RMD bulge, widow's-penalty exposure (the survivor files single at compressed brackets), legacy to high-bracket heirs under the 10-year rule, and simple current-vs-future bracket arbitrage.
5. **Execution is a hard route:** size and educate here; execute with the advisor/CPA (withholding vs estimated payments, in-kind vs cash, intra-year timing).

## Social Security claiming
- Claim window 62–70. Early claiming permanently reduces the benefit per the SSA reduction schedule; delaying earns ≈8%/year from FRA (67 for those born 1960+) to 70, landing 24–32% above the primary insurance amount depending on cohort.
- Breakeven for delaying typically falls in the low-to-mid 80s; longevity expectations and survivor needs dominate the decision, not breakeven alone.
- Survivor optimization: the higher earner's record becomes the survivor benefit — the higher earner delaying is longevity insurance for the couple.
- Spousal benefit: up to 50% of the worker's PIA at the spouse's own FRA; no delayed credits accrue on spousal benefits.
- The earnings test applies only before FRA; SSA recomputes the benefit at FRA to credit withheld months — it defers benefits rather than destroying them.

## Medicare timing traps
- Working past 65 with employer coverage (20+ employees): the SEP protects you, but COBRA and retiree coverage do NOT count as current-employment coverage — the 8-month SEP clock starts when active employment/coverage ends.
- HSA conflict: enrolling in Medicare (including up to 6 months of retroactive Part A) ends HSA eligibility; plan the contribution stop date when delaying enrollment past 65 (VERIFY the retroactivity rule before a specific answer).
- Both Part B and Part D late penalties are lifetime. Full rules and 2026 figures live in `2026-quick-reference.md`.

## HSA as a retirement vehicle
Triple tax advantage (deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawals). Max strategy: pay current medical costs out of pocket, invest the HSA, and keep receipts — qualified reimbursement has no deadline. After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA with no penalty. Coordinate with the Medicare stop-contribution rule above.

## Backdoor & mega-backdoor Roth
- **Backdoor:** nondeductible traditional-IRA contribution → Roth conversion. The pro-rata rule aggregates ALL traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRA balances at year-end (Form 8606); pre-tax IRA money makes the conversion partially taxable. Standard fix: roll pre-tax IRA balances into a 401(k)/Solo 401(k) first.
- **Mega-backdoor:** after-tax 401(k) contributions above the deferral limit, up to the $72,000 (TY2026) §415(c) ceiling, then in-plan Roth conversion or in-service rollover — only if the plan document allows both features. A Solo 401(k) can be drafted to allow it.

## NUA, 72(t), and harvesting
- **NUA:** distribute appreciated employer stock in-kind in a qualifying lump-sum distribution; ordinary tax on cost basis now, LTCG treatment on the appreciation at sale. Mechanics are unforgiving and irreversible — hard route for execution.
- **72(t)/SEPP:** penalty-free pre-59½ withdrawals via substantially equal periodic payments; locked for the longer of 5 years or until 59½; modification retroactively voids the exception. Hard route.
- **Tax-loss harvesting:** realized losses offset gains plus up to $3,000/year of ordinary income; the wash-sale rule disallows the loss if a substantially identical security is bought within 30 days before or after — across accounts, including IRAs. **Tax-gain harvesting:** realize gains inside the 0% LTCG bracket in low-income years; in WA, also check position against the state capital-gains standard deduction.

## Charitable strategies
Donor-advised funds (bunch several years of giving into one itemizing year; fund with appreciated stock for a fair-market-value deduction plus no capital gain); QCDs (70½+, up to $111,000 TY2026, direct from IRA, satisfies RMD dollars, and is excluded from AGI — an AGI exclusion beats a deduction for IRMAA and Social Security-taxation purposes); appreciated-stock gifts to charities directly. TY2026 changes the math: non-itemizers get $1,000/$2,000, and itemizers face a new 0.5%-of-AGI floor — re-run bunching analysis under the new rules.

## Insurance frameworks
- **Life:** replace income through the dependency years plus debts and education funding; term insurance for the need window; permanent insurance only for permanent needs (estate liquidity, special-needs planning). Product purchase is a hard route.
- **Disability:** protects the largest asset (remaining lifetime earnings); prefer own-occupation definitions.
- **Umbrella:** liability coverage at least at net-worth scale, layered above auto/home limits.
- **Long-term care:** traditional vs hybrid (life/LTC) tradeoffs; for WA residents, net the WA Cares benefit (~$36,500 lifetime) against the real exposure — it is a partial offset, not coverage (see `washington.md`).

## Cash and emergency funds
3–6 months of essential spending as the norm; size toward the top or beyond with variable or self-employment income (the default household here has both W-2 and SE income). Hold in HYSA/money market/T-bills as its own bucket outside the investment allocation.
